How it works

Evolution strategies

An evolver is the loop: it decides who breeds, who survives, and when to stop. GET has two, and the choice between them changes more than it looks like it should — including whether your fitness comparisons are fair.

What an evolver does

Both strategies keep a population of genomes and repeat the same three moves: score them, select parents from the better ones, and replace some individuals with their children. They differ in how much of the population turns over at once. 

pub trait Evolver<G: Genome> {
    type TypeContext;
    fn new(shared: SharedEvolutionContext<G>, type_context: Self::TypeContext, population: Vec<G>) -> Self;
    fn run<F: Fitness>(&mut self, fitness: &F, seed: u64) -> EvolutionOutcome<G>;
}
The evolver does not build genomes, deliberately. Constructors differ per representation and some are fallible, so a generic evolver cannot mint a G. The dispatch layer builds the starting population — where genome-specific knowledge already lives — and hands it in. That keeps run's signature clean, surfaces bad dimensions at startup rather than mid-run, and makes evolvers directly testable from a hand-built population.

Every value has one source of truth. The shared context carries only the genome context, the rates, max_mutations and the selection strategy. Population size is population.len(). Network size and edge cap belong to the genome context. Config still carries all three, but only dispatch reads them — a second copy on the context could drift, and nothing would report the disagreement.

Selection

Tournament selection: draw a handful of individuals at random, and the best one wins the right to be a parent. tournament_size is how many entrants — larger means stronger selection pressure, because a weak individual has to get luckier to win.

Ordering is by fitness with ties broken by lower index, so a tournament's outcome depends only on which indices were drawn and not on the order the random number generator produced them.

There are two draw methods, and they sample differently, on purpose:

MethodSamplingUsed by
select(count)With replacementgenerational
tournament_indices()Without replacementsteady-state

The steady-state one has no choice: it feeds tournament-local replacement, and "the worst two members" is undefined over a multiset. The generational one keeps the textbook default. The divergence means one tournament_size implies marginally different pressure per strategy — about 4.90 expected distinct entrants at k = 5 on a population of 100, roughly 2%, which is noise against a stochastic run.

One consequence for generational: two picks can be the same individual, so crossover between a genome and its own clone does nothing but mutate. Steady-state cannot self-mate by construction.

Generational

The whole population is replaced each generation. Score everyone, log a row, then build an entirely new population from the old one's winners.

The rules that are easy to get wrong

  • Elites are rescored every generation, like everyone else. They are copied forward unchanged, not exempted from scoring. Under a stochastic objective an elite's recorded fitness therefore moves between generations while its genome does not — and that is correct. The new number is a fresh sample of the same individual, and freezing the old one would let a lucky draw persist forever.
  • Odd slot counts lose one child. Crossover yields two children, but population_size - elite_count may be odd. The last pair contributes one child and the other is discarded. Only the final pair is ever affected.
  • Generation 0 is the initial population. It is logged before any breeding happens, so both strategies' histories share an axis.

Steady-state

One mate-and-replace event at a time. Most of the population simply persists. Each event draws a single tournament of distinct individuals; its two best breed, and the two children overwrite that same tournament's two worst.

What tournament-local replacement buys

PropertyBecause
Self-elitist The tournament's best is never among the replaced, so the population's best is never discarded — and no explicit elitism is needed.
Diversity-preserving A globally poor individual survives until it happens to be drawn.
Cheap O(k log k) per event rather than an O(population) scan for the global worst — which matters at 100,000 events.
Unconditional A child takes its slot even if it scores worse than what it displaces. The best-never-discarded guarantee comes from the tournament structure, not from a comparison.
tournament_size ≥ 4 is required here, and only here. Two parents and the two individuals they replace must be distinct. Three still preserves the tournament's best but makes the second parent one of the replaced; two breaks self-elitism outright, since both parents are replaced by their own children. Generational has no such floor.

Logging cadence

Logging every event would give a 100,000-row history, so steady-state logs the starting population as iteration 0 and then one row per population_size events — a "generation equivalent". So history.len() == num_mating_events / population_size + 1. The iteration-0 row is what makes a log self-contained; without it, a run shorter than one interval produced nothing at all.

Choosing between them

GenerationalSteady-state
TurnoverEveryone but the elites, every generation Two individuals per event
Scoring per stepThe whole populationThe two new children only
ElitismExplicit, via elite_count Implicit, from the tournament structure
Population-level parallelismAs wide as the population Two-way, regardless of cores
With a Python objectiveOne crossing per generation One crossing per event — a poor fit
Fitness comparisonsFair — everyone rescored under one seed Carries stale fitnesses — see below
Known limitation, accepted: steady-state carries stale fitnesses. It scores only the two new children per event, so the epidemic dice advance per event. The fitness values sitting in the population were computed under different dice than the children being compared against them — and an individual that drew lucky dice keeps its inflated score for the rest of the run, because nothing rescores it. Generational is immune: it rescores everyone each generation under one seed.

This is inherent to combining incremental scoring with a stochastic objective; the alternatives each trade it for a different distortion rather than removing it. Prefer generational when the objective is stochastic and the comparison needs to be fair — which, with any of the three SIR objectives, it is.

What a run reports

The reported best is the best of the final population, for both strategies — not a running best carried across generations. Two reasons this is the honest number rather than a limitation:

  • Fitness is stochastic between batches, so a running best would be substantially a record of which generation drew the luckiest sample — the same argument that rejects freezing an elite's old score.
  • At elite_count = 0, which is permitted, a non-elitist generational GA genuinely can lose its best individual. The run really did end without it, and a report claiming otherwise would describe a population that no longer exists. At elite_count = 0 the divergence is a property of your configuration, not of the report.

Both strategies return the identical graph for a given winner, since expression is deterministic. Generational takes it out of the graphs its last scoring pass already built; steady-state re-expresses it, having no such vector to hand. The difference is cost, not behaviour.

Reproducibility is the same for both: each seeds ChaCha8Rng from run's seed — not the standard generator, whose algorithm may change between releases of its library, which would defeat the entire purpose of a seed argument. See Seeds & reproducibility, and Logs & results for the columns.
