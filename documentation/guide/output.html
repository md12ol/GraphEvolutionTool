How it works

Logs & results

Every run produces two things: a convergence log showing how the population improved, and the best individual it found. This page is what the columns mean and which units they are in.

The convergence log

One row per logged iteration — every generation for generational, and every population_size mating events for steady-state. Both log the starting population as iteration 0, so the two strategies' histories share an axis.

ColumnIs
iteration Generation number, or mating-event number.
best_fitness Best individual in the population at that iteration.
mean_fitness Population mean.
std_dev Population standard deviation — divides by n. These are all the individuals there are, not a sample of some larger group, so a single individual has deviation zero.
ci_95 planned 95% confidence interval half-width on the mean: 1.96 · s / √n, where s is the sample deviation — divides by n-1.
seed, run planned The run's seed and its replicate index, repeated on every row.
The two denominators disagree on purpose. std_dev describes the population as the complete thing it is, so it divides by n. A confidence interval is inherently a sampling statistic and needs the sampling denominator to mean anything, so ci_95 divides by n-1. Reporting both is fine; computing the interval from the population deviation would not be. A single individual has std_dev = 0 and an undefined ci_95, which is reported as 0.
ci_95 is a within-population band, per iteration. It answers "how tightly is this generation clustered around its mean". It is not the band you usually see on a published convergence plot — that one is mean best-fitness over n replicates with a spread showing run-to-run variability, and it is computed when replicate logs are aggregated, not here. Both are legitimate and they answer different questions. The seed and run index on every row are what make the second one computable.

Why the seed and run index are on every row

So that 30 replicate logs can be concatenated into one file and still be separable. It costs two columns and removes the need for a naming convention, a directory layout, or a parse-the-filename step in whatever plots them.

The best individual

PieceIs
the genome The winning genome, printed. This is what Genome::print exists for — it lets a non-generic entry point record which individual won without knowing its representation.
the network The graph it expressed, as a weighted edge list of (u, v, multiplicity) triples with u < v. Pairs with no edge are not listed.
the fitness Its score, in your objective's own units.

The reported best is the best of the final population, for both strategies — not a running best carried across generations. The reasoning, including why that is the honest number rather than a limitation, is on Evolution strategies.

Which units am I looking at?

Everything inside the engine is in engine orientation — lower is better, whatever your objective was. The Python boundary converts on the way out:

ValueConverted on the way out?
best_fitness, mean_fitness Yes — you see them in your objective's units and sign.
std_dev, ci_95 No, and correctly so: a spread is identical under negation.

So an epi_spread log reads in infected nodes and goes up; an epi_prof_match log reads as an RMSE and goes down. Full explanation of the two number systems on Fitness & orientation.

Reading EvolutionOutcome directly from Rust gives you engine-oriented numbers. That is the stated cost of converting only at the edges, and it is what the direction stored on the outcome is for. If you are embedding GET rather than driving it from Python, convert yourself.

Getting the data out planned

results = evolver.run(seed=20260812, runs=30, max_cores=8)

best = max(results, key=lambda r: r.best_fitness)   # epi_spread maximizes
print(best.best_fitness)
print(best.best_edges)
print(best.best_genome_repr)

for row in best.history:
    print(row.iteration, row.best_fitness, row.mean_fitness, row.std_dev, row.ci_95)

evolver.save_logs("convergence.csv")     # every row of every replicate
evolver.save_results("results.txt")      # best individual + the generated TOML

The generated TOML is written alongside the results as the provenance record of what produced them. With the seed on every log row, that pair is everything needed to re-run the experiment exactly.

Plotting a convergence curve

Once the history is available, the usual figure is three lines and a band:

import csv
from collections import defaultdict

rows = list(csv.DictReader(open("convergence.csv")))

# within-run: one line per replicate
by_run = defaultdict(list)
for r in rows:
    by_run[r["run"]].append((int(r["iteration"]), float(r["best_fitness"])))

# across-run: the band you usually publish — mean over replicates at each iteration,
# which is a DIFFERENT number from the ci_95 column beside it
by_iter = defaultdict(list)
for r in rows:
    by_iter[int(r["iteration"])].append(float(r["best_fitness"]))

iterations = sorted(by_iter)
mean_best  = [sum(by_iter[i]) / len(by_iter[i]) for i in iterations]

Note the comment: aggregating best_fitness across replicates is the across-run band, and it is not the ci_95 column. Mixing them up produces a plot that looks right and answers the wrong question.
